## SECTION 1: SYSTEM OVERVIEW AND OPERATING PRINCIPLES

The liquid cooling system maintains the internal combustion engine at its optimal thermal efficiency range (typically 195°F to 220°F or 90°C to 105°C). 

The primary components comprise:
- Radiator: Transfers heat from circulating engine coolant to ambient airflow.
- Thermostat: Temperature-sensitive wax pellet valve controlling coolant flow between engine block and radiator.
- Water Pump: Centrifugal impeller driven by the serpentine belt or timing belt that circulates coolant.
- Radiator Pressure Cap: Maintains system pressure (typically 13 to 16 PSI / 0.9 to 1.1 bar), raising coolant boiling point by approximately 45°F (25°C).
- Electric Cooling Fans: Pulls air through radiator cores at idle or low vehicle speeds, modulated by the Powertrain Control Module (PCM).
- Coolant Expansion Reservoir: Accommodates thermal expansion and contraction of fluid.

## SECTION 2: ENGINE OVERHEATING DIAGNOSIS & CAUSES

Engine overheating occurs when heat generated by internal combustion exceeds the heat dissipation capacity of the cooling system. 

### Primary Causes of Engine Overheating:
1. Low Coolant Level: Caused by external leaks (radiator hoses, water pump weep hole, heater core, radiator seams) or internal leaks (blown cylinder head gasket, cracked engine block or cylinder head).
2. Thermostat Stuck in Closed Position: Prevents hot coolant in the engine cylinder head from reaching the radiator for cooling. The upper radiator hose will remain cold or lukewarm while the engine temperature rises rapidly into the red zone.
3. Electric Cooling Fan Inoperation: Fan motor failure, blown cooling fan fuse, defective fan relay, or malfunctioning coolant temperature sensor (ECT) preventing fan engagement when coolant exceeds 205°F (96°C) or when A/C is activated.
4. Faulty Radiator Pressure Cap: If the cap's spring-loaded pressure relief valve weakens or its rubber seal degrades, system pressure drops, causing coolant to boil at lower temperatures (around 212°F / 100°C) and overflow into the expansion tank.
5. Clogged Radiator: Internal mineral scale deposits (from using tap water) or external debris (leaves, road grime, bugs) blocking radiator cooling fins.
6. Water Pump Failure: Worn bearings causing shaft wobble, eroded impeller vanes unable to generate sufficient hydraulic flow, or loose drive belt slippage.
7. Blown Cylinder Head Gasket: Combustion gases entering cooling passages, indicated by rapid bubbling in the radiator neck, white exhaust smoke with sweet odor, and milky oil emulsion on the oil filler cap.

## SECTION 3: COOLING SYSTEM INSPECTION & TESTING PROCEDURES

### Step-by-Step Diagnostic Routine:
1. Cold System Visual Check: NEVER open the cooling system while the engine is hot. Inspect coolant level in the overflow bottle. Check condition of upper and lower radiator hoses for softness, swelling, or brittle cracking.
2. Cooling System Pressure Test: Install a hand pressure tester onto the radiator neck. Pump to the specified cap rating (14–16 PSI). Observe pressure gauge for 2 minutes:
   - If pressure drops more than 2 PSI without visible external puddle, inspect for internal head gasket leak or heater core seepage.
3. Radiator Cap Pressure Test: Mount radiator cap onto the pressure tester adapter. Pump until release; verify release occurs within ±1 PSI of stamped rating.
4. Thermostat Function Test: Start cold engine with radiator cap installed. Monitor upper radiator hose temperature with an infrared pyrometer. The hose should stay relatively cool until the engine reaches ~190°F (88°C), at which point it should rapidly heat up as the thermostat opens.
5. Bleeding Air Pockets: Trapped air causes localized boiling and erratic temperature gauge spikes. Raise front of vehicle slightly, attach a spill-free funnel, fill with 50/50 ethylene glycol coolant, and run engine with heater on MAX heat until all air bubbles cease.
